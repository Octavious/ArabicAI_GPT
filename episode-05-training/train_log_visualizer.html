Loss curves — is your model learning?

Loss measures how "surprised" the model is by the correct next token. Lower is better. At initialization, loss should be near ln(vocab_size) — that's the surprise of a model guessing uniformly at random. For a 4,096-token vocabulary, that's about 8.32. Each drop of 1.0 in loss means the model's predictions became roughly 2.7× more concentrated on the right answer.

The shape you want is a steep initial drop (the model learns the easy statistics of language: common words, letter patterns, punctuation) followed by a long gentle decline as it learns finer structure. Sharp later-stage drops are suspicious; flat plateaus mean learning has stalled.

Train loss and val loss should move together. healthy They track each other closely. overfitting Train keeps dropping while val flattens or rises — the model is memorizing rather than generalizing.

Train vs val gap — is it generalizing?

This is train_loss − val_loss at each evaluation. It tells you whether the model is learning real patterns (things that also work on unseen data) or just memorizing the training set.

A growing positive gap (train much lower than val) is the classic signature of overfitting — the model is getting very good at stories it has seen but no better at new ones. A stable gap near zero is what you want, and a negative gap (val lower than train) is common early in training because the reported train loss is averaged over the whole window, including worse early steps.

For a small model on TinyStories, we expect gaps between −0.1 and +0.1 throughout training. If you see a persistent gap beyond +0.2, that's a signal to reduce model capacity, add more data, or increase dropout.

Learning rate — warmup and cosine decay

The learning rate is how big a step the optimizer takes when adjusting each parameter. Too big and training explodes; too small and learning stalls.

The shape here has two phases. The warmup (first few hundred steps) linearly ramps the learning rate up from zero to its peak — this prevents instability at the start when weights are still random. The cosine decay smoothly drops it back down, letting the model take smaller and smaller steps as it homes in on a good minimum.

Think of it like adjusting a dial: you crank it hard at first to get in the general neighborhood, then dial in finer and finer corrections as you get close to the answer. A flat learning rate throughout training would either start too unstable or end too coarse.

Perplexity — the intuitive quality metric

Perplexity is just e^loss — the same information as the loss curve but on a scale humans can actually interpret. A perplexity of N means the model's predictions are roughly as accurate as picking from N equally likely options. A random model on a 4,096-token vocabulary has perplexity 4,096.

You can read improvement here as intuition: going from perplexity 4,000 to 40 means the model has narrowed the field 100×. Going from 40 to 4 means another 10×. Each halving of perplexity typically costs more training than the last — which is why the curve bends sharply at first and flattens later.

For a well-trained small model on TinyStories, final perplexity in the 4–6 range is excellent. GPT-2-small, trained on much richer data, sits around 35 on generic English; our simpler domain explains why we can reach much lower.
